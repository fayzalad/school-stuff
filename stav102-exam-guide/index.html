Unit 1Introduction and General Concepts

Notes §1 · Lecture 1 · Tutorial Quiz 1. Low on long-question marks, high on MCQ marks — roughly 4–6 MCQs come straight from here in both past papers.

1.1–1.2 The language of statistics

Statistics is the field of study which deals with the collection, summarising, presentation, analysis and interpretation of data. Data is any information (usually values) observed and collected from a source; a collection of data is a data set.
TermDefinition (learn this wording)Symbol
VariableAny characteristic of interest on which data is collected and analysed. Its value varies between subjects.X
PopulationThe collection of all possible data values that exist for the variable under study.—
SampleA subset of data values drawn from a population.—
Population sizeThe number of items/observations in the population.N
Sample sizeThe number of items/observations in the sample.n
Population parameterA measure that describes a characteristic of a population.μ, σ, σ²
Sample statisticA measure that describes a characteristic of a sample.x̄, s, s²
The relationship MCQ. Both past papers ask a version of this:
  • "The relation between a statistic and a sample is the same as the relation between …" → a parameter and a population.
  • "The relation between a parameter and a population is the same as the relation between …" → a statistic and a sample.
Think of it as: statistic : sample :: parameter : population. Both start with the same letter as their partner — statistic/sample, parameter/population.

1.3 The two components of statistics

Descriptive statistics

Methods used to organise, summarise and present data in an informative way. Two forms:

  • Numerical values (mean, standard deviation …)
  • Tables and graphs (frequency table, histogram …)

→ Units 2 and 3 of this course.

Inferential statistics

A group of methods used to draw inferences (conclusions) about a population based on sample information.

  • Estimation
  • Hypothesis testing
  • Forecasting, modelling

→ Later units.

The distinguishing test. Ask: did the conclusion go beyond the data that was actually collected?
  • "In our sample of 123 gym members, 32% used supplements." → descriptive (it only describes the sample).
  • "Approximately 35% of all gym members use supplements." → inferential (it generalises to the population).
Quick check · Quiz 1 Q1

What is statistical inference?

  • The process of drawing conclusions about a population based on a parameter
  • The process of drawing conclusions about a statistic based on a parameter
  • The process of drawing conclusions about a sample based on population data
  • The process of drawing conclusions about a population based on sample data
Inference always runs sample → population. Options that run the other way (population → sample) describe the logic backwards. A parameter is already a population fact, so "conclusions about a population based on a parameter" isn't inference at all.

1.4.1–1.4.2 Types of variables and data types

VARIABLE | +----------------+----------------+ | | QUALITATIVE QUANTITATIVE (categorical) (numerical) values are names/labels values are numbers e.g. gender, race, | marital status, +----------+----------+ mode of transport | | DISCRETE CONTINUOUS whole numbers only any value in a range (0,1,2,3,...) (1.66 m, 74.3 kg) e.g. number of e.g. height, weight, students, number time, temperature of car accidents
Qualitative (categorical) variables measure categorical or descriptive attributes. Formally: a variable is qualitative if the values observed cannot be described using numbers — they take on values that are names or labels.
Quantitative variables measure quantitative attributes whose magnitude can be expressed numerically. They take on numerical values.
Discrete: observations are whole numbers (countable). You cannot observe 3.5 students.  ·  Continuous: observations can take on any value within a range.
The classic trap. A variable that looks numeric can still be qualitative. A driver's licence code (EB, C1) and the first three digits of a cellphone number are labels, not quantities — you cannot meaningfully average them. Conversely, age among a list of "type of vehicle, gender, age, licence code" is the only quantitative one. Ask yourself: does arithmetic on this value mean anything?

1.4.3 The four measurement scales

ScaleData typeKey propertyExamples
NominalQualitativeCategories of equal importance, no ranking. Cannot be added, subtracted, multiplied or divided.Gender; city of residence; race; type of vehicle; marital status
OrdinalQualitativeCategories with an implied ranking — they can be placed in order — but gaps between ranks are not meaningful.Clothing size (XS,S,M,L,XL); income category (lower/middle/upper); service rating (excellent/good/fair/poor); Likert scales
IntervalQuantitativeDifferences are meaningful, ratios are not, and zero is not absolute (zero doesn't mean "none").Temperature in °C or °F; calendar years; IQ scores
RatioQuantitativeDifferences and ratios are meaningful, and zero is meaningful (means absence of the quantity).Height; weight; age; income in Rand; time taken; distance; number of items
How to decide in 5 seconds:
  1. Is it a name/label? → Nominal.
  2. Is it a label with a natural order? → Ordinal.
  3. It's a number. Does zero mean "none of it"?  Yes → Ratio. No → Interval.
Temperature in °C is the standard interval example, and it appears in both past papers: 0 °C does not mean "no temperature", and 20 °C is not "twice as hot as" 10 °C. But note that time taken to complete a race is ratio — zero seconds means no time elapsed.
Quick check · 2025 Test Q2

The temperature (in °C) in an air-conditioned room would involve measurement on a(n) ______ scale.

  • Nominal
  • Ordinal
  • Interval
  • Ratio
  • None of the above
Differences are meaningful (2 °C to 5 °C is the same gap as 100 °C to 103 °C), but 0 °C does not mean the absence of temperature and 20 °C is not twice 10 °C. That's the definition of interval.
Quick check · Quiz 1 Q5

Which of the following is an example of an ordinal variable?

  • Your annual expenditure on clothing
  • The rating (excellent, good, fair or poor) you would give a fast food chain

Expenditure is ratio (numeric, meaningful zero). Favourite TV programme is a label with no natural order → nominal. The first three digits of a phone number are a label → nominal. Only the rating has categories that can be ranked: excellent > good > fair > poor.

Worked example · The parameter/statistic/population/sample question

Question (2025 Test, Q5–7). The Centre for Energy Research wants to investigate the average wind speed (m/s) in the Gqeberha region. A sample of 500 wind-speed measurements was collected over June–July 2025. The average wind speed for the sample was 5.23 m/s.

Identify (i) the population, (ii) the sample, (iii) the parameter of interest, (iv) the statistic of interest.

Method — work through the four in this order:

  1. Variable: wind speed (m/s) in the Gqeberha region.
  2. Population: all wind-speed measurements in the Gqeberha region. (Everything you could have measured.)
  3. Sample: the sample of 500 wind-speed measurements — the subset actually collected.
  4. Parameter: a measure describing the population. They want to know the average wind speed (of the whole region) — an unknown, un-numbered quantity.
  5. Statistic: a measure describing the sample. That is the average wind speed of 5.23 m/s — a known number, computed from the sample.
The give-away: the parameter is the one without a number attached (you don't know it — that's why you sampled). The statistic is the one with the number (5.23). If two options look identical apart from a number, the numbered one is the statistic.
Quick check · Quiz 1 Q10

The government wishes to estimate the true number of adults in South Africa who are illiterate. A random sample of 10 000 adults was interviewed and 1 107 were found to be illiterate. What is the parameter of interest?

  • The number of the 10 000 sampled who are illiterate
  • The number of adults in South Africa who are illiterate
  • The number of adults in South Africa
  • The total number of people in South Africa
The parameter describes the population characteristic they are trying to estimate — the illiteracy count among all SA adults. Option 1 (1 107) is the statistic. Options 3 and 4 describe the population's size, not the characteristic under study.

2.1.2–2.1.3 Pie charts and bar charts

Pie chart — steps
  1. Calculate the angle of each category: (f/n) × 360
  2. Divide a circle into segments using those angles
  3. Label the graph (title + category labels or legend)
Bar chart — steps
  1. Horizontal axis (x): the categories
  2. Vertical axis (y): frequency or percentage
  3. Draw a vertical bar per category to the height of its frequency
  4. Label both axes
Bar chart vs histogram — the visual difference. A bar chart has gaps between the bars (categories are separate). A histogram has no gaps (the intervals are continuous). That's why Practical 1 tells you to set the Excel gap width to 0% for a histogram.

2.2.1 Quantitative frequency tables — Sturges' formula

This is the highest-value procedure in Unit 2. It appears as a long question in both past papers and as 2–3 MCQs.

Step 1 · Number of class intervals k = 1 + 3.3 log(n)   =   1 + 3.3 ln(n)ln(10)   → ROUND UP
Step 2 · Class width c = xmax − xmink   → ROUND UP
  1. Number of classes. k = 1 + 3.3 log(n). Always round up to the next whole number — even 5.02 becomes 6.
  2. Class width. c = (xmax − xmin) / k, using the rounded-up k. Always round up to the next whole number.
  3. Lower boundary of the first class. If xmin is an integer, use xmin itself. If xmin is not an integer, use the first integer smaller than xmin.
  4. Build the classes. Upper boundary = lower boundary + c. The next class starts where the previous one ended. Continue for all k classes.
  5. Count the frequencies. Use tally marks. [ means inclusive, ) means exclusive — so a value of 15 belongs to [15–19), not to [11–15).
  6. Add the extra columns the question asks for: relative frequency, percentage, cumulative frequency (F), relative cumulative frequency, class midpoint (x).
The two rounding traps — this is where marks are lost most often.
  • Round UP, never to nearest. k = 6.487 → 7 (not 6). c = 5.7 → 6 (not 5.7, not 6 by "rounding to nearest" luck — by the rule). This exact question cost you 2 marks in Tutorial Quiz 2 Q2.
  • Use the rounded k in step 2, not the unrounded one. If k = 8.181 → 9, then c = (xmax−xmin)/9, not /8.181 and not /8.

2.2.2–2.2.4 Histogram, ogive and frequency polygon

Histogram

Bars of frequency (or relative frequency) over each class interval.

  • x-axis: class boundaries, consecutive, no gaps between bars
  • y-axis: frequency
  • Shows where data is clustered and the shape
Ogive

A line graph of cumulative frequency. Tells you how many values lie above or below a particular value.

  • Plot F against the UPPER limit of each class
  • Starts at 0 opposite the lower limit of the first class
  • Ends at n (or 100%) at the upper limit of the last class
Frequency polygon

Unit 3Descriptive Statistics: Numeric

Notes §3 · Lectures 4, 5 & 6 · Tutorial Quiz 3 · Practical 2. This is the heaviest unit in the test. In the 2025 paper it carried 24 of the 30 long-question marks.

Before anything else, fix these two words in your head:
  • Ungrouped data = raw data, a list of individual values.
  • Grouped data = data already organised into a frequency distribution table with class intervals.
Every measure in this unit has two different formulas — one for each. Reading the question to identify which form you have is worth more than any single formula. If you see a list of numbers → ungrouped. If you see a table with class intervals → grouped.

Three aspects of a data set are investigated:

Central location

Where the data lies on the number line. Mean, median, mode.

Dispersion (spread)

How far the data is from the centre. Range, variance, standard deviation, CV.

Relative standing

The position of a value relative to the rest. Quartiles, percentiles.

3.2 Measures of central location

3.2.1–3.2.2 The mean

Ungrouped — sample mean x̄ = 1n Σi=1n xi = Σxin
Grouped — sample mean x̄ = Σ fixiΣ fi = Σ fixin

where xi is the class midpoint and fi is the class frequency.

Population vs sample notation. The population mean is μ and divides by N; the sample mean is x̄ and divides by n. Same arithmetic, different symbol — and MCQs test whether you know which is which. The population/sample distinction becomes a real arithmetic difference for the variance (n−1 vs N).

3.2.3–3.2.4 The median

The median is the value such that half the observations are smaller and half are larger — the middle point of the data when arranged in ascending order. Unlike the mean, it is not affected by extreme outlying values.
Ungrouped — 2 steps memorise
  1. Order the data ascending.
  2. Median position = (n+1)/2. Take the value in that position.

If n is odd, the position is a whole number → read off that value. If n is even, the position ends in .5 → take the average of the two values either side.

Grouped — formula on sheet
Me = li + c (n/2 − Fi−1)fi

Median class = the first class whose cumulative frequency ≥ n/2.
li = lower boundary of median class · c = width of median class · Fi−1 = cumulative frequency of the class before it · fi = frequency of the median class

Worked example · Median, ungrouped (odd and even n)

3.2.5–3.2.6 The mode

The mode (Mo) is the most frequently occurring value. It is the only central-location measure that works for categorical as well as numeric data. One mode = unimodal; two = bimodal; more than one = multimodal (and you must state all of them).
Ungrouped
  1. Order the data
  2. Identify the most frequent observation
Grouped on sheet
Mo = li + c (fi − fi−1)(fi − fi−1) + (fi − fi+1)

Modal class = the class with the highest frequency. fi−1 and fi+1 are the frequencies of the classes immediately before and after it.

3.3 Measures of dispersion

3.3.1 Range

Range = xmax − xmin

Simplest measure. For 12, 15, 15, 15, 16, 22, 23, 25, 28: Range = 28 − 12 = 16. It's easy but crude — a single extreme value distorts it completely.

3.3.2–3.3.3 Variance and standard deviation

The standard deviation (s) gives an indication of the average deviation of each data point from the mean. The variance is s². Crucially: s is in the original units of measurement; s² is in units squared. If the data is in Rands, s is in Rands and s² is in Rands² (hard to interpret) — which is why standard deviation is usually preferred for reporting.
Ungrouped — definitional s² = Σ(xi − x̄)²n − 1
Ungrouped — computational (USE THIS ONE) s² = Σxi² − 1n(Σxi)²n − 1
Grouped — computational s² = Σfixi² − 1n(Σfixi)²n − 1        s = √ s²
Always use the computational formula. The definitional formula makes you subtract the mean from every single value and square it — slow, and if x̄ isn't a round number your rounding errors compound. The computational formula needs only two totals: Σx and Σx² (or Σfx and Σfx²). Most calculators will give you both directly in stats mode.
n − 1 vs N — a guaranteed MCQ. Dividing by n − 1 means you are computing a sample variance (it makes the estimator unbiased). Dividing by N means a population variance. 2019 MCQ Q10 asks exactly this: "If the variance is correctly computed with n−1 in the denominator, which is true?" → The data set is a sample. Unless a question explicitly says "population", use n − 1.

3.3.4 Coefficient of variation

Coefficient of variation CV = sx̄ × 100   (a percentage, and unitless)
The CV is a measure of relative variability — the standard deviation expressed as a percentage of the mean. Because it has no units, it allows you to compare the variability of two data sets measured in different units or on very different scales. The higher the CV, the greater the dispersion relative to the mean.
Worked example · Using the CV to compare two investments (notes §3.3.4)

Jabu is choosing between two investments:

  • VDO Corp: mean earnings return R250 per share, standard deviation R100.
  • TBS Unit Trusts: mean return 12%, standard deviation 7%.

You cannot compare R100 with 7% directly — different units entirely. That's exactly what the CV is for.

CV(VDO) = (100/250) × 100 = 40%
CV(TBS) = (7/12) × 100 = 58.333%

Conclusion. Although VDO's standard deviation looks much larger in absolute terms (R100 vs 7%), relative to its mean the TBS returns are more variable (58.333% > 40%). Since higher variability means higher risk, Jabu should invest in VDO Corp.

Always add the interpretation sentence. Questions say "determine and interpret the coefficient of variation" — a bare number gets half the marks. Say: "The standard deviation is X% of the mean, indicating [high/low/moderate] relative variability."

3.4 Measures of relative standing

These measure where a particular value sits relative to the rest. If you score 58% but that beats 95% of the class, relative standing is what tells you so.

3.4.1 Quartiles, ungrouped

Quartiles divide an ordered data set into quarters.
  • Q1 — 25% of the data lies below it; splits off the lowest 25% from the highest 75%.
  • Q2 — cuts the data in half. Q2 is the median.
  • Q3 — 75% of the data lies below it; splits off the lowest 75% from the highest 25%.
  1. Order the data ascending.
  2. Calculate the position:   Q1 at (n+1)/4  ·  Q2 at (n+1)/2  ·  Q3 at 3(n+1)/4
  3. Read off, or interpolate. If the position is a whole number, take that observation. If it is w.d (w = whole part, d = decimal part), interpolate:
    Q = xw + d × (xw+1 − xw)
    where xw is the wth value and xw+1 is the next one.

Q1 = li + c (n/4 − Fi−1)fi Q1 class = first class with F ≥ n/4

3.4.3–3.4.4 Percentiles

Percentiles divide the data into hundredths. The pth percentile Pp is the value below which p percent of the data lies. So 68% of the data is below P68. Note that P25 = Q1, P50 = Q2 = Median, P75 = Q3.
Ungrouped memorise

Position of Pp = p(n+1)/100, then read off or interpolate exactly as for quartiles.

Grouped on sheet
Pp = li + c (np/100 − Fi−1)fi

Pp class = the first class with F ≥ np/100.

The positions are different for ungrouped and grouped — do not mix them up.
Ungrouped uses (n+1) in the position: p(n+1)/100, (n+1)/4, (n+1)/2.
Grouped uses n in the target count: np/100, n/4, n/2, 3n/4.
That "+1" only exists in the ungrouped world. Getting it wrong shifts every answer.

3.5 Box-and-whisker plots and outliers

A box-and-whisker plot displays the five-number summary: Lower Limit, Q1, Median, Q3, Upper Limit. From one you can read the key values, spot outliers, and judge whether the data is symmetric or skewed and in which direction.
Interquartile range (IQR) contains the middle half of the data and measures the spread of the middle 50%:
IQR = Q3 − Q1
Lower limitLL = Q1 − 1.5 × IQR
Upper limitUL = Q3 + 1.5 × IQR
LL Q1 Me Q3 UL | ┌──────┬───────┐ | ●----+-----------┤ │ ├-------------+----● | └──────┴───────┘ | whisker the box whisker ○ ○ OUTLIER ←──── IQR ────→ OUTLIER (below LL) (above UL)
  1. Determine the median, Q1, Q3 and IQR.
  2. Draw a number line. Mark Q1, the median and Q3.
  3. Draw a box from Q1 to Q3, with a vertical line inside at the median.
  4. Compute LL = Q1 − 1.5·IQR and UL = Q3 + 1.5·IQR.
  5. Draw the whiskers out to LL and UL. Plot any values beyond them individually — those are the outliers.
Outliers are data points that differ significantly from the other observations. Any value less than LL or greater than UL is an outlier. Some outliers are genuine natural variation; others come from data-entry errors or equipment malfunctions, and those distort analyses.
How to answer "Is there an outlier? Motivate your answer." for full marks. Three components:
  1. State Q1, Q3 and IQR with values.
  2. Compute LL and UL explicitly.
  3. Compare: name the specific value(s), say whether they fall outside [LL, UL], and conclude in a sentence: "Yes, 26 is an outlier because 26 > UL = 21.125."
A bare "yes" earns nothing. The word "motivate" means the marks are in the reasoning.

Probability is a numerical measure of the chance that a particular event will occur or not. It takes a value between 0 and 1 (and can be expressed as a percentage). 0 = the event cannot occur (impossible event). 1 = the event is certain. A value close to 1 means a greater chance; close to 0, a smaller chance. We write P(A) for the probability that event A occurs.

4.3 The three approaches to assigning probability

Classical

If an experiment has n equally likely simple events, the probability of a specific outcome is 1/n.
Rolling a 5 on a fair die = 1/6.

Relative frequency

Probability = the outcome's relative frequency over many repetitions: P(A) = f/n.
850 of the last 1000 customers bought → P = 0.85.

Subjective

The probability reflects the degree to which we believe the outcome will occur. Used when outcomes aren't equally likely and there's no history.
The chance your business survives a recession.

In practice — mutually exclusive, equally likely outcomes P(A) = n(A)n(S)    where n(A) = number of outcomes in A,  n(S) = number of outcomes in S
Requirements of probability (a standard MCQ):
  1. 0 ≤ P(Ei) ≤ 1 for every simple event
  2. Σ P(Ei) = 1 across the whole sample space
So "which of these is not a valid probability value?" → anything negative or greater than 1. Watch the disguises: √1.14 ≈ 1.068 > 1 is invalid, while 25/25 = 1 ✓, (−1)² = 1 ✓ and e−2 ≈ 0.135 ✓ are all fine.

4.5 Rules of probability

Addition rule on sheet P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)
Addition rule when A and B are mutually exclusive P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B)   (because P(A ∩ B) = 0)
Complement rule memorise P(Ā) = 1 − P(A)
Why you subtract the intersection. If you just add P(A) + P(B), everything in the overlap gets counted twice — once as part of A and once as part of B. Subtracting P(A ∩ B) removes the double-count. When the events are mutually exclusive there is no overlap to remove, which is why the rule simplifies.

Worked example · Sample space, events and P(A ∪ B) (2019 Test Q3)

Two coins are tossed.

(a) List the sample space S.

S = {HH, HT, TH, TT}   →  n(S) = 4

(b) List the sample points in each event.

A: at least one head is observed → A = {HH, HT, TH}   n(A) = 3
B: at least two heads are observed → B = {HH}   n(B) = 1

(c) Determine P(A ∪ B).

First note that A ∩ B = {HH}, so P(A ∩ B) = 1/4.

P(A) = 3/4 = 0.75  ·  P(B) = 1/4 = 0.25  ·  P(A ∩ B) = 1/4 = 0.25

P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B) = 0.75 + 0.25 − 0.25 = 0.75

Check by listing: A ∪ B = {HH, HT, TH}, so P(A ∪ B) = 3/4 = 0.75 ✓

Note that B is entirely inside A (every outcome with at least two heads certainly has at least one head), so the union is just A. When one event is a subset of the other, A ∪ B = the bigger one and A ∩ B = the smaller one. Spotting this saves time.

4.5.3 Conditional probability

Often the probability of an event is influenced by whether a related event has already occurred. P(A|B) reads "the probability of A given that B has occurred".
Conditional probability on sheet P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B)P(B)   (provided P(B) ≠ 0)         P(B|A) = P(A ∩ B)P(A)
Getting the denominator right is the whole game. The thing after the vertical bar is what you are given — and it goes in the denominator. "Given that it is corporate, what is P(South Africa)?" → P(SA | C) = P(SA ∩ C)/P(C). Reversing it (dividing by P(SA)) is the most common probability error in the whole test. Read the sentence twice and underline the "given" clause before you write anything.
Worked example · Conditional probability from percentages (notes §4.5.3)

60% of STAV102 students major in Accounting. 55% major in both Accounting and Law. Find the probability that a randomly selected student majors in Law given that they major in Accounting.

Step 1 — define the events precisely (this earns marks on its own):

Let A = the event that the selected student is majoring in Accounting
Let B = the event that the selected student is majoring in Law

Step 2 — translate the given information:

"60% major in Accounting" → P(A) = 0.6
"55% major in both" → P(A ∩ B) = 0.55
"Law given Accounting" → we need P(B|A)

Step 3 — substitute:

P(B|A) = P(A ∩ B)/P(A) = 0.55/0.6 = 0.917
Note the answer (0.917) is bigger than P(A ∩ B) = 0.55. That's normal — conditioning shrinks the sample space to just the students taking Accounting, so the proportion goes up. If your conditional probability comes out smaller than the joint probability, you have divided by the wrong thing.

The two-way table method — your best tool

Most probability questions in this course describe a survey with two categorical variables. Build the two-way table first, fill in every cell, then read the answers straight off. This turns a hard problem into arithmetic.

  1. Draw a 2×2 (or larger) table with row totals, column totals and a grand total.
  2. Write in every number the question gives you, in the correct cell.
  3. Fill in the gaps by subtraction — rows and columns must add to their totals.
  4. Read off answers:
    • P(A) = the row/column total for A ÷ grand total
    • P(A ∩ B) = the single cell where A and B meet ÷ grand total
    • P(A ∪ B) = (row total + column total − cell) ÷ grand total
    • P(A|B) = the cell ÷ B's total  — you no longer divide by the grand total
The shortcut worth memorising: for a conditional probability from a table, you never need the grand total at all. P(A|B) = (cell where A and B meet) ÷ (total of the B row or column). The "given" event's total becomes your new denominator.

4.6 Dependent and independent events

Two events are dependent when the occurrence of one influences the probability of the other. They are independent when it does not.
Test for independence memorise A and B are independent  ⟺  P(A|B) = P(A)  or  P(B|A) = P(B)  or  P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B)

4.6.1 The multiplication rule

General (dependent) on sheet P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B|A)
= P(B) × P(A|B)
Independent events on sheet P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B)
Do not confuse the two "P(A ∩ B) = …" lines on your formula sheet. P(A)·P(B) is only valid when the events are independent. If the question involves sampling without replacement, or an explicit conditional relationship, you must use P(A)·P(B|A).

Worked example · The 8-ball container, both ways (notes §4.6.2–4.6.3)

A container has 8 identical balls: 5 blue and 3 red. Define Bi = blue on the ith draw, Ri = red on the ith draw.

(a) Two balls WITH replacement — both red.
The ball goes back, so the second draw is unaffected: independent, and P(R2) = 3/8 both times.

P(R1 ∩ R2) = P(R1) × P(R2) = (3/8)(3/8) = 9/64 = 0.141

(b) Two balls WITH replacement — one red and one blue.
"One of each" can happen two ways: red-then-blue, or blue-then-red. These are mutually exclusive, so add them.

P((R1∩B2) ∪ (B1∩R2))
 = P(R1)P(B2) + P(B1)P(R2)
 = (3/8)(5/8) + (5/8)(3/8) = 15/64 + 15/64 = 30/64 = 0.469

(c) Two balls WITHOUT replacement — both red.
After drawing one red, only 2 reds remain out of 7 balls: dependent.

P(R1 ∩ R2) = P(R1) × P(R2|R1) = (3/8)(2/7) = 6/56 = 0.107

(d) Two balls WITHOUT replacement — one red and one blue.

= P(R1)P(B2|R1) + P(B1)P(R2|B1)
= (3/8)(5/7) + (5/8)(3/7) = 15/56 + 15/56 = 30/56 = 0.536
Sanity pattern: without replacement, "both the same colour" is always less likely and "one of each" always more likely than with replacement — because removing a ball makes the remaining pool more mixed. Use this to check your answers.

4.7 Counting techniques

When the sample space is too big to list, use counting rules to find n(A) and n(S), then apply P(A) = n(A)/n(S).

Basic counting rule memorise If step 1 can be done in m1 ways, step 2 in m2 ways, …, step k in mk ways,
the whole experiment can be done in  m1 × m2 × ⋯ × mk  ways.
Factorial memorise n! = n × (n−1) × (n−2) × ⋯ × 3 × 2 × 1   arrangements of n distinct objects   (0! = 1)
Combinations on sheet — order does NOT matter nCr = n!r!(n − r)!
Permutations MEMORISE — not on sheet nPr = n!(n − r)!
Combination or permutation? Ask one question: does the ORDER of the selection change the outcome?
  • No → combination. Selecting 3 students for a sample — ABC is the same sample as BAC. Lottery numbers. A committee. A hand of cards.
  • Yes → permutation. Awarding 3 prizes of different value to 3 of 5 students — being picked first means the most valuable prize. Assigning lanes. Ranking. Passwords.
And note nPr is always ≥ nCr (by a factor of r!), because each combination can be ordered in r! ways. From 5 students choosing 3: 5C3 = 10 but 5P3 = 60 = 10 × 3!.

5.1 Random variables

Random variable (X) — a rule that assigns a number to every outcome of a random experiment. Capital letter = the variable (X); lower-case = a value it takes (x).
Discrete
Countable values (0, 1, 2, …). Each value gets its own probability.
e.g. number of heads, number of red lights, number of defects.
Continuous
Any value in a range. P(X = x) = 0 — probabilities only for intervals (areas under a curve).
e.g. height, time, mass, income.

5.4 Binomial distribution

Binomial experiment — properties (learn these; "list three properties" is a common 3-mark question)
  1. Fixed number of trials, n.
  2. Each trial has only two outcomes: success or failure.
  3. The probability of success, p, is constant from trial to trial (q = 1 − p).
  4. The trials are independent.
X = number of successes in n trials, x = 0, 1, …, n. Write X ~ BI(n, p).
Binomial — given on the formula sheetP(X = x) = nCx · px · (1 − p)n − x     E(X) = np     V(X) = np(1 − p)
How to spot it: "n people/items/trials", "x% of …", "each independently", "probability p of …". There is a maximum (you can't have more successes than n).
Calculator: nCr is usually SHIFT + ÷ on a Casio fx-82/991.

5.5 Poisson distribution

Poisson experiment — properties
  1. Counts the number of (rare) events in a fixed interval of time, distance, area or volume.
  2. The number of events in one interval is independent of the number in any other (non-overlapping) interval.
  3. The probability of an event is the same for intervals of equal size, and proportional to the size of the interval.
  4. The probability of two or more events in a very small interval is ≈ 0 (rare events).
X = number of events per interval, x = 0, 1, 2, … (no upper limit). Write X ~ Poisson(λ).
Poisson — givenP(X = x) = e−λ λxx!     E(X) = V(X) = λ

5.6–5.7 Continuous distributions and the normal distribution

Probability density function f(x): f(x) ≥ 0 and the total area under the curve = 1. f(x) is not a probability, and P(X = x) = 0.

Properties of the normal distribution X ~ N(μ, σ²):

  • Bell-shaped and symmetric about μ (mean = median = mode).
  • Completely defined by its two parameters μ and σ².
  • Tails approach the horizontal axis but never touch it.
  • Empirical rule: ≈ 68% within μ ± σ, ≈ 95% within μ ± 2σ, ≈ 99.7% within μ ± 3σ.

6.1–6.3 The idea

Sampling distribution — the distribution of a statistic (x̄, p̂, s², …) over all possible samples of the same size n from the same population. Different samples give different x̄'s; the sampling distribution describes how they vary.
Parameter vs statistic
Parameter describes the population (μ, σ, p). Statistic describes a sample (x̄, s, p̂) and is used to estimate the parameter.
Sampling error
The difference between a statistic and the parameter it estimates, e.g. x̄ − μ. It happens because we only see a sample. Larger samples → smaller sampling error.

The die example in the notes: population μ = 3.5, σ² = 2.92. All 36 samples of size 2 give a sampling distribution of x̄ with mean 3.5 (= μ) and variance 2.92/2 = 1.46. Standard error = √1.46 = 1.208.

6.4 The Central Limit Theorem

Central Limit Theorem. If x̄ is the mean of a random sample of size n from any population with mean μ and variance σ², then the sampling distribution of X̄ is approximately normal for sufficiently large n (usually n ≥ 30), with
μx̄ = μ      σ²x̄ = σ²n      σx̄ = σ√n  (the standard error)
Population is…Then X̄ is…
Normalexactly normal for any n
Not normal (or unknown)approximately normal if n is large (n ≥ 30) — that's the CLT
Standardising a sample mean — givenZ = X̄ − μσ / √n
The question that decides everything: one value or an average?
"A randomly selected customer / bottle / student" → use σ.
"The average / mean of a sample of 25" → use σ/√n.
Exams love asking both in the same question, (i) then (ii).

As n increases: the mean of X̄ stays at μ, the spread σ/√n shrinks, and the shape becomes more bell-shaped.

7.1 Point vs interval estimates

Point estimate
A single number: x̄ estimates μ, p̂ estimates p, s² estimates σ².
Interval estimate (CI)
A range that contains the parameter with a stated confidence level 1 − α. α = significance level. Common: 90%, 95%, 99%.

More confidence ⇒ wider interval. A 99% CI is wider than a 95% CI from the same data.

8.1 The language

Hypothesis — a claim about a population parameter (μ, p, σ², μ₁ − μ₂). Never about x̄ or p̂.
H₀ (null) — status quo / no change / no difference. Always contains "=".
H₁ (alternative) — what the researcher is trying to show. Contains >, < or ≠.
Conclusions are only ever "Reject H₀" or "Do not reject H₀" — never "accept H₀".
Reject H₀Do not reject H₀
H₀ trueType I error — probability αCorrect decision
H₀ falseCorrect decisionType II error — probability β
Type I (α)
Rejecting a true H₀. Court analogy: convicting an innocent person. Considered the worse error, so we fix α small (1%, 5%, 10%). α = significance level = size of the test.
Type II (β)
Not rejecting a false H₀ — letting a guilty person go free. Power of a test = 1 − β. Calculating β is beyond this course.
If no significance level is given, use α = 0.05 (stated in the notes).

Stating the hypotheses — translate the words

Right-tailed  H₁: >Left-tailed  H₁: <Two-tailed  H₁: ≠
greater than, more than, above, higher, longer, bigger, increased, exceeds, improved (if bigger = better)less than, below, lower, shorter, smaller, decreased, reduced, faster (time)different from, changed, not equal to, not the same as, "is there a difference"
"is at least" → H₀ claim, test H₁: <"is at most" → H₀ claim, test H₁: >"equal to", "the same as"
Traps that lose the hypothesis mark:
  • Writing x̄ or p̂ in the hypotheses (H₁: x̄ > 30 000 is wrong — use μ).
  • Using the sample value instead of the claimed value (H₁: μ > 28 when the claim is 25 — μ₀ is always the claimed/historical value).
  • Variance vs standard deviation: "standard deviation greater than 200 g" ⇒ H₁: σ² > 200² = 40 000 (or σ > 200). Keep them consistent with the test statistic.
  • "Faster reaction time" means a smaller time ⇒ H₁: μnew < μold.

The six steps — "this is six marks" (Lecture 17)

  1. State the hypotheses H₀ and H₁ (in symbols, with the right parameter). Lecturer's tip: work out H₁ first from the question's wording, then H₀ is the "=" version.
  2. State the significance level α.
  3. Calculate the test statistic (formula, substitution, answer to 3 dp).
  4. Find the critical value (with its subscript, e.g. t24; 0.995 = 2.797). Drawing the curve with the rejection region shaded makes step 5 obvious.
  5. Decision — state the rejection rule, compare, "reject H₀" / "do not reject H₀".
  6. Conclusion in words — "At the 5% level of significance, there is sufficient / insufficient evidence to conclude that [H₁ in the context of the question]."
TestReject H₀ if (z test)Reject H₀ if (t test)
Right-tailed (H₁: >)zts > z1−αtts > tdf; 1−α
Left-tailed (H₁: <)zts < −z1−αtts < −tdf; 1−α
Two-tailed (H₁: ≠)|zts| > z1−α/2|tts| > tdf; 1−α/2
Conclusion template — memorise it word for word:
Reject: "At a 5% level of significance, there is sufficient evidence to conclude that the average delivery time is less than 20 days."
Do not reject: "At a 5% level of significance, there is insufficient evidence to conclude that the average delivery time is less than 20 days."
The conclusion always restates H₁ in words — never "H₀ is true".

Reading the t and χ² tables

t table
Row = df. Column = lower-tail probability: 1 − α for one-tailed, 1 − α/2 for two-tailed (and CIs).
e.g. two-tailed α = 0.05, n = 20 ⇒ row 19, column 0.975 ⇒ 2.093.
df not in the table (e.g. 43)? Use the nearest smaller df (40) — it's the cautious choice.
The last row (∞) equals the z values.
χ² table
Row = df = n − 1. Columns are lower-tail areas: small columns (0.005–0.10) on the left, large (0.90–0.995) on the right.
Right-tailed test α = 0.05 ⇒ column 0.95. Left-tailed ⇒ column 0.05. Two-tailed ⇒ both 0.025 and 0.975.

Unit 9Regression and Correlation Analysis

Notes pp. 133–143 · Lectures 21–22 · Practical 6 · Unit 9 solutions. On the past finals this unit was a long question worth 5–8 marks plus 4–5 MCQs, usually from an Excel output.

9.1–9.2 The idea and the scatter plot

Regression and correlation look at the linear relationship between two quantitative variables measured on the same items, recorded as pairs (xi, yi). This is the third part of statistics, statistical modelling (after descriptive and inferential).

Independent variable x (explanatory / predictor): the one you use to predict. Goes on the horizontal axis.
Dependent variable y (response): the one you want to predict. Goes on the vertical axis.
How to tell which is x: read the question for “estimate y using x” or “predict y from x”. Swapping them changes a, b and every prediction (but not r).

A scatter plot is always the first step. Its pattern is one of three kinds:

Positive linear
Points rise from left to right: as x increases, y increases. r > 0, b > 0.
Negative linear
Points fall from left to right: as x increases, y decreases (brightness ↑, battery life ↓). r < 0, b < 0.
No linear relationship
A shapeless cloud (or a curve). r ≈ 0.

9.3 The least-squares regression line

The method of least squares fits the straight line ŷ = a + bx that is closest to the points. With one x it is simple linear regression.

All on the formula sheet SXX = Σx² − (Σx)²n    SYY = Σy² − (Σy)²n    SXY = Σxy − (Σx)(Σy)n

b = SXYSXX     a = ȳ − b x̄     ŷ = a + bx
b — the slope
The change in ŷ for every one-unit increase in x. Its sign gives the direction: b > 0 positive relationship, b < 0 negative. Interpretation sentence: “For every additional [unit of x], [y] increases (decreases) by b [units of y] on average.”
a — the y-intercept
The predicted y when x = 0. Only meaningful if x = 0 is sensible and inside the data range.

The table method (do it this way in the exam)

  1. Make five columns: x, y, x², y², xy. Add each column. (y² is only needed for r.)
  2. Compute x̄ = Σx/n and ȳ = Σy/n.
  3. Compute SXX, SXY (and SYY).
  4. b = SXY/SXX, then a = ȳ − b x̄. Keep b unrounded when you calculate a.
  5. Write the line with the variable names: e.g. pricê = 219.926 + 9.012 × age.

9.3.3 Predicting and extrapolation

Substitute the new x into the line: ŷ* = a + bx*. Answer with units and a sentence.

Extrapolation means predicting for an x outside the range of the data used to fit the line (below the smallest or above the largest x). The notes say it should always be avoided: the line is only trusted inside min(x) ≤ x* ≤ max(x). An MCQ may ask which prediction is extrapolation.
Worked example — phone brightness and battery life (notes 9.3.1)

n = 6, Σx = 363, Σy = 54.1, Σxy = 2826.6, Σx² = 25 937, x̄ = 60.5, ȳ = 9.0167.

SXX = 25 937 − 363²/6 = 3 975.5  ·  SXY = 2826.6 − (363)(54.1)/6 = −446.45
b = −446.45/3975.5 = −0.1123  ·  a = 9.0167 − (−0.1123)(60.5) = 15.8108
ŷ = 15.8108 − 0.1123x. At 45% brightness: ŷ = 15.8108 − 0.1123(45) = 10.757 hours

45% lies inside the data range (28% to 100%), so this is not extrapolation. Predicting at 10% brightness would be.

9.4 The correlation coefficient r and the coefficient of determination r²

On the formula sheetr = SXY√(SXX SYY)

The population correlation is ρ (rho); the sample estimate is r (Pearson’s correlation coefficient). It measures the direction and strength of the linear relationship.

Facts examined in MCQs
  • −1 ≤ r ≤ 1 (it can never be greater than 1)
  • r = 1: perfect positive linear; r = −1: perfect negative linear
  • r = 0: no linear relationship
  • r can be negative (between 0 and −1)
  • r has the same sign as b
  • swapping x and y doesn’t change r
Interpreting the size (notes table)
|r|Strength
0.00 – 0.35weak
0.35 – 0.70moderate
0.70 – 1.00strong
Sentence: “There is a strong positive linear relationship between [x] and [y].” Always state strength + direction + linear.
Coefficient of determination r² = r × r, between 0 and 1, usually given as a percentage. It is the proportion of the total variation in y that is explained by the variation in x (by the regression line). Sentence: “r² = 0.81, so 81% of the variation in monthly sales is explained by advertising spend.” The other 19% is due to other factors.
Correlation is not causation. A strong r shows the variables move together, not that x causes y.

Unit 10Time Series Analysis

Notes pp. 144–154 · Lecture 23 + “Of moving averages” (centred MA) video · Unit 10 solutions. On both 2025 finals this unit was 2–3 MCQs: one component question and two moving-average calculations. They are quick marks if you know the method.

10.1 What a time series is

Time series: a set of observations of one quantitative variable, ordered in time (minutes, days, quarters, years). It relates two variables: time and the quantity measured.

Why we study it: mainly forecasting (predicting future values from past behaviour, the primary objective), plus business planning (comparing actual with expected performance), studying past behaviour, and comparing variables over time or place.

10.2 The four components

1 · Trend (T)
The smooth, long-term general direction: increasing, decreasing or stable. E.g. electricity demand rising from 1985 to 2007.
2 · Cyclical (C)
Up-and-down movements around the trend that last longer than a year, e.g. business cycles. Their length depends on the industry.
3 · Seasonal (S)
A pattern that repeats every year (month, week, day) because of the season: higher electricity use every winter, retail peaks every December. Only visible in quarterly, monthly, weekly… data.
4 · Irregular / random (I)
Unpredictable variation with no pattern or regular period (strikes, floods, one-off events). What is left after removing T, C and S.
MCQ trap (January 2026, Q33): “Which is not a component of a time series?” The options include correlation variations, which is not a component. Memorise the four: trend, cyclical, seasonal, irregular.

10.3.1 Moving averages: smoothing out the random part

To forecast we reduce the random variation by smoothing. A k-point moving average is the mean of k consecutive values, then the window slides one step and is averaged again.

Not on the formula sheet — learn it3-point MA at t = yt−1 + yt + yt+13     5-point MA at t = yt−2 + yt−1 + yt + yt+1 + yt+25
  • Each average is written next to the middle value of the group it averages.
  • A 3-point MA has no value for the first and last time point. A 5-point MA loses two at each end.
  • The smoothed series shows mostly the trend; seasonal and random wiggles are reduced.

Top exam traps — all units

The ones that cost marks in your quizzes, tests and the past-paper memos.

Units 1–4

  1. Sturges k and width c: always round UP (k = 5.29 → 6; c = 5.7 → 6). Round k first, then compute c with the rounded k.
  2. Raw vs grouped positions: raw data uses (n+1): (n+1)/2, (n+1)/4, p(n+1)/100. Grouped formulas use n: n/2, n/4, np/100.
  3. Fi−1, not Fi, in the grouped median, quartile and percentile formulas: the cumulative frequency of the class before.
  4. Σx² vs (Σx)², and f·x² means f × (x²).
  5. Grouped mean divides by n = Σf, not by the number of classes.
  6. Outlier questions need the reasoning: IQR, LL = Q₁ − 1.5·IQR, UL = Q₃ + 1.5·IQR, compare, conclude.
  7. “Interpret” the percentile: “30% of light bulbs lasted less than 16.156 days.”
  8. Addition rule: subtract P(A∩B) unless the events are mutually exclusive.
  9. Independence test: check P(A∩B) = P(A)P(B). From P(A∪B): P(A∩B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A∪B).
  10. Without replacement: the second probability changes (P(K then Q) = 3/24 × 8/23).
  11. Counting: order matters ⇒ nPr; it doesn’t ⇒ nCr; repeated trials ⇒ multiply (3 dice ⇒ 6³ = 216).
  12. Measurement scales: temperature in °C is interval (no true zero); counts, time, mass and money are ratio.

Units 5–8

  1. N(μ, σ²) gives the variance: N(80, 4²) ⇒ σ = 4.
  2. Rescale Poisson λ to the interval asked: 6 per 3 hours ⇒ 2 per hour ⇒ 18 per 9 hours.
  3. “At least / at most / more than / fewer than” for discrete X, and use the complement for “at least one”.
  4. One item uses σ, an average uses σ/√n. Proportion questions use √(p(1−p)/n).
  5. z or t? This course uses z when σ is known or n ≥ 30; t with n − 1 df only when σ is unknown and n < 30.
  6. Two-tailed ⇒ α/2 in the critical value (95% ⇒ 1.96).
  7. Proportion test uses p₀ in the standard error; the proportion CI uses p̂.
  8. χ² tests use s² and σ₀²: “sd less than 0.7” ⇒ σ₀² = 0.49.
  9. Paired or independent? Same people before/after ⇒ paired.
  10. Step 6 needs a sentence in context; write “do not reject H₀”, never “accept H₀”.
  11. Sample size: always round up.

Units 9–10

  1. x and y the right way round (“predict y from x”).
  2. Correlation test df = n − 2.
  3. r interpretation = strength + direction + “linear”; r² = “% of the variation in y explained by x”.
  4. Don’t extrapolate: predictions outside the x-range are unreliable.
  5. Centre even moving averages and average the correct pair.
  6. Four components only: trend, cyclical, seasonal, irregular.
  7. 3 decimals on every final answer; keep 4+ in the working.
